University of Auckland research prototype · Jul–Oct 2026

Energy-Harvesting Backpack

A backpack whose load moves up and down as you walk, and that motion turns a generator. Walking at 4 km/h, the prototype averaged 55 mW into a 3.3 kΩ load, above the project's 30–50 mW target, and 81 mW when hand-cranked.

My role: I joined the research group in July 2026 and took the suspended-load platform from CAD to a working, wearable prototype: mechanical design, laser cutting, 3D printing, welding the generator support and assembly. I also wired every coil on the generator and traced and fixed a wiring fault. The generator itself was provided by the research group; the platform, the coil wiring and the 21 October power test are mine.

How it works

  • The rear plate is fixed to the backpack frame and follows the wearer's torso.
  • The front plate carries the load and slides vertically on two guide rods.
  • Walking makes the load plate move relative to the fixed frame.
  • A rack on the moving plate drives a pinion coupled to the research group's generator.
  • Elastic bands support the load in the current build, replacing an earlier spring layout.

Why this layout

A direct rack-and-pinion path makes displacement easy to observe, keeps the generator accessible and lets the plates, guides, elastic elements and drive be changed during testing.

The same layout carried the project through assembly, fault-finding and the final power test without a change to the overall operating principle.

Fault-finding: no generator output

  1. Symptom. After assembly, the generator produced no output at the terminals.
  2. Method. The coils are wired together to one output, so a single break anywhere stops the whole circuit. I checked continuity with a multimeter, joint by joint along the coil chain.
  3. Cause. A coil lead had been worn through by friction against an internal contact surface.
  4. Result. After the repair, the output came back.

What I learned

In a rotating electromechanical assembly, wiring is a mechanical design problem too. Leads that pass near moving or rubbing surfaces need routing and protection designed in from the start.

Power test, 21 October 2026

Walking at 4 km/h gave 55 mW against the project's 30–50 mW target. I set up, ran and analysed the test myself.

  • Load. Generator output across a fixed 3.3 kΩ resistor.
  • Capture. Every run recorded on a SIGLENT SDS1204X-E oscilloscope: hand-cranked, then worn on a treadmill at 4 and 5 km/h.
  • Analysis. RMS voltage, power and energy calculated point by point from the raw samples, including every low point of the AC waveform.

What the 5 km/h run showed

  • About 205 mW averaged over the first 39 s during more vigorous movement. Contact with the end stops was suspected, but not confirmed.
  • About 50 mW over 70 s once I deliberately calmed the movement, still at the top of the target range.
  • About 105 mW averaged over the whole two-minute recording.
  • Takeaway: how freely the load moves matters as much as walking speed.

Test conditions

Fixed 3.3 kΩ resistive load; power calculated from the measured voltage; one recorded run per condition; treadmill speeds are set values. These are generator-side AC load measurements, not regulated USB output or conversion efficiency. Reported total apparatus mass was about 2.5 kg; moving mass and walking displacement were not separately logged. A load sweep to find the best resistance is the next step.

Next steps (Semester 1, 2027)
