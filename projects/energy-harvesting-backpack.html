Suspended-Load Energy-Harvesting Backpack

I joined this University of Auckland research project after earlier students had explored suspended-load backpack mechanisms and the research group had developed a bidirectional rotating electromagnetic generator. I designed and built the suspended-load platform shown below: the full backpack CAD and mechanical design, laser-cut acrylic plates, 3D-printed parts, a welded generator support structure, and all of the wiring on the group's generator prototype. I also participated in doctoral-researcher-led testing and device demonstrations.

How the Mechanism Works

  • The rear plate is fixed to the backpack frame and follows the wearer's torso.
  • The front plate carries the suspended load and translates vertically on two guide rods.
  • Walking creates relative motion between the fixed frame and moving load plate.
  • A rack attached to the moving plate drives a pinion coupled to the group's existing generator.
  • Elastic bands support the payload in the current physical build, replacing the earlier spring-layout concept.

Why I Chose This Layout

The first prototype needs to answer basic questions before adding more complexity. A direct rack-pinion path makes displacement easy to observe, keeps the generator interface accessible and allows the plates, guides, elastic elements and drive interfaces to be changed during testing.

It is a research prototype, not a finished backpack product. Comfort, durability, harvested power and walking performance still require quantified, repeatable measurements. A phone registering charging through energy storage does not establish a sustained charging rate or net harvested power.

How the Work Moves Forward

This project depends on communication, not only CAD. I review mechanism choices with my supervisor and previous students, then speak with technicians in different university labs and workshops about cutting, printing, assembly access and practical tolerances. Their feedback becomes the next CAD revision or test.

Fault-Finding: When the Generator Gave No Output

  1. Symptom. After assembly, the generator produced no output at the terminals.
  2. Method. The coils are wired together to one output, so a single break anywhere kills the whole circuit. I checked continuity with a multimeter joint by joint along the coil chain instead of guessing.
  3. Cause. A coil lead had been worn through by friction against an internal contact surface.
  4. Result. After the repair, the output came back.

What It Taught Me

In a rotating electromechanical assembly, wiring is a mechanical design problem too. Leads that pass near moving or rubbing surfaces need routing and protection that are designed in, not added after a failure.

The Prototype in Use

Design Evidence

Verification Boundary

What is real now My CAD, fabrication, wiring and physical platform build are complete evidence. Volt-level open-circuit voltage was observed at the coil terminals in team bench tests, and the device demonstrations were led by doctoral researchers with my participation.
What is still open No personal claim of measured efficiency, sustained output power, comfort improvement or durability is made. The deck's 0.1-1 W values are targets, and cited literature results belong to other work.

Next Validation Steps

  1. Document repeatable travel, friction, alignment and hard-stop checks on the assembled platform.
  2. Compare elastic-band settings under matched excitation.
  3. Separate generator output from energy delivered by the storage stage.
  4. Check support and electrical pickup reliability over repeated motion.
  5. Add representative payloads and record displacement during controlled walking tests.
  6. Measure electrical output only after the mechanical motion is repeatable.
