Suspended-Load Energy-Harvesting Backpack

I joined this University of Auckland research project after earlier students had explored suspended-load backpack mechanisms and the research group had developed a bidirectional rotating electromagnetic generator. I took the backpack from CAD to a working, wearable prototype and then proved it on the oscilloscope myself. I designed and built the suspended-load platform shown below: the full backpack CAD and mechanical design, laser-cut acrylic plates, 3D-printed parts, a welded generator support structure, and all of the coil wiring on the group's generator prototype. On 21 October 2026 I ran the power tests: walking at 4 km/h with the backpack on, it delivered 55 mW average into a 3.3 kΩ load, above the project's 30–50 mW target, and 81 mW when hand-cranked.

How the Mechanism Works

  • The rear plate is fixed to the backpack frame and follows the wearer's torso.
  • The front plate carries the suspended load and translates vertically on two guide rods.
  • Walking creates relative motion between the fixed frame and moving load plate.
  • A rack attached to the moving plate drives a pinion coupled to the group's existing generator.
  • Elastic bands support the payload in the current physical build, replacing the earlier spring-layout concept.

Why I Chose This Layout

The first prototype was built to answer basic questions before adding more complexity. A direct rack-pinion path makes displacement easy to observe, keeps the generator interface accessible and allows the plates, guides, elastic elements and drive interfaces to be changed during testing.

That choice paid off: the same layout carried the project through assembly, fault-finding and the final power tests without a redesign.

This project depended on communication, not only CAD. Their feedback became the next CAD revision or test.

Fault-Finding: When the Generator Gave No Output

  1. Symptom. After assembly, the generator produced no output at the terminals.
  2. Method. The coils are wired together to one output, so a single break anywhere kills the whole circuit. I checked continuity with a multimeter joint by joint along the coil chain instead of guessing.
  3. Cause. A coil lead had been worn through by friction against an internal contact surface.
  4. Result. After the repair, the output came back.

What It Taught Me

In a rotating electromechanical assembly, wiring is a mechanical design problem too. Leads that pass near moving or rubbing surfaces need routing and protection that are designed in, not added after a failure.

Power Test, 21 October 2026: Target Beaten

I wired the generator output across a 3.3 kΩ resistor, captured every run on a SIGLENT SDS1204X-E oscilloscope, then walked on a treadmill with the backpack on. I calculated RMS voltage, power and energy point by point from the raw samples rather than reading the screen, and kept every valley in the AC waveform, so the averages are honest averages. The project target was 30–50 mW; walking at 4 km/h gave 55 mW.

What the 5 km/h run showed

  • About 205 mW averaged over the first 39 s, when I moved vigorously and the load swung to its end stops.
  • About 50 mW over 70 s once I deliberately calmed the movement, still at the top of the target range.
  • About 105 mW averaged over the whole two-minute recording.
  • The lesson: how freely the payload moves matters as much as walking speed. That is exactly what I will tune next.

Test conditions

Fixed 3.3 kΩ resistive load; power calculated from the measured voltage; one recorded run per condition; treadmill speeds are set values. A load sweep, which should push the numbers higher, is the next step.

Next Semester: Pushing the Output Higher

  1. Sweep the electrical load to find the resistance that extracts the most power.
  2. Tune the elastic-band stiffness so the payload swings freely without hitting its end stops.
  3. Log payload mass, displacement and belt speed alongside the voltage, and repeat each speed.
